# Flat rate vs reducing balance: what's the real difference?

A 3% flat rate and a 3% reducing-balance rate are not the same loan. The flat one costs a lot more.

A **flat rate** charges interest on the full amount you borrowed for the whole loan, even though you pay some of it back every month. A **reducing-balance rate** charges interest only on what you still owe. That one difference is why a bank can advertise a small number and still collect a lot of interest.

## How each one is worked out

**Flat rate:** interest = amount × rate × years. Add that to the amount borrowed and divide by the number of months.

Take RM100,000 over 9 years at 3% flat.
- Interest: RM100,000 × 3% × 9 = RM27,000
- Monthly payment: (RM100,000 + RM27,000) ÷ 108 months = **RM1,175.93**

**Reducing balance:** each month the bank charges interest on the balance that is left. As you pay the loan down, the interest part shrinks.

## The same payment, two different numbers

That RM1,175.93 a month is exactly what you would pay on a loan of RM100,000 over 9 years at about **5.5% reducing balance**. So "3% flat" and "5.5% reducing" are one deal described in two ways.

| Loan | Quoted as flat | Monthly payment | Total interest | Same deal as reducing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RM100,000, 9 years | 3.0% | RM1,175.93 | RM27,000.00 | about 5.5% |
| RM100,000, 5 years | 4.0% | RM2,000.00 | RM20,000.00 | about 7.4% |

Now compare the second line with a plain 4% reducing-balance loan on the same RM100,000 for 5 years: RM1,841.65 a month and about RM10,499 in interest. Quoting 4% flat instead of 4% reducing costs you roughly RM9,500 more.

As a rough feel, the real rate behind a flat rate often comes out close to double. In the two examples it is 1.8 to 1.9 times, but it depends on how long the loan runs, so don't rely on the rule. Work it out.

## Why people get caught

A flat rate looks small on the poster. Nothing on it tells you that you are paying interest on money you have already returned. Some older agreements also used the "Rule of 78" for early settlement, which loads the interest toward the start of the loan, so paying off early saved less than people expected.

## What changed for car loans

News reports (Paultan, The Star) say the Hire Purchase (Amendment) Act 2026 came into force on 1 June 2026. For hire purchase, which is how most car loans work, flat rates and the Rule of 78 are replaced by reducing-balance interest and a quoted effective interest rate (EIR). A transition period was mentioned, so an older agreement may still use the old method.

I could not find anything that says whether the same applies to personal loans, so for those, ask the bank which kind of rate it is quoting.

## How to compare two offers

1. Ask for the **effective interest rate (EIR)** and the **total amount you will repay**. Those two numbers compare any two loans.
2. If you only get a flat rate, work out the total: monthly payment × number of months. Subtract the amount borrowed. That is the interest you pay.
3. Don't compare a flat rate with a reducing rate. Convert one of them first.

You can try this yourself in the [loan calculator](/loan-calculator). Switch "The rate is" between *flat* and *reducing balance* and watch the monthly payment and total interest change.

*These are estimates for explanation, not financial advice. Your bank's offer and its own figures are what count.*
